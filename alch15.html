Notes

1. Intermolecular forces and chemical bonds

Intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules. They differ from covalent bonds, which hold atoms together within a molecule. Ionic bonding describes electrostatic attraction between ions, often in a giant lattice rather than in discrete molecules.

The three main attractions considered here are London dispersion forces, permanent dipole-dipole attractions, and hydrogen bonds. During ordinary boiling of a molecular substance, intermolecular attractions are overcome; the molecules' covalent bonds remain intact.

2. London dispersion forces

Fluctuations in electron density can create an instantaneous dipole. This distorts a neighbouring particle's electron cloud, producing an induced dipole. The correlated opposite partial charges attract.

Instantaneous dipole → Induced dipole → Attraction
  • These dipoles constantly fluctuate; they are not permanent.
  • London forces occur between atoms and molecules, including both polar and non-polar molecules. They remain present alongside other intermolecular forces.
  • Larger, more polarizable electron clouds generally give stronger dispersion forces. Shape and contact area also affect their overall strength.
  • Noble-gas boiling points generally rise down the group because the electron clouds become more polarizable and the London attractions become stronger.

3. Permanent dipoles and molecular shape

A permanent dipole is a persistent separation of partial positive and negative charge. In HCl, chlorine attracts the bonding electrons more strongly, so H is δ+ and Cl is δ−.

When suitably oriented, the δ+ region of one polar molecule attracts the δ− region of another. Like partial charges repel. Polar molecules also experience London dispersion forces.

  • HCl: polar; has permanent dipole-dipole attractions.
  • CO2: linear and symmetric; its two bond dipoles cancel.
  • CCl4: symmetric tetrahedral structure; its bond dipoles cancel.
  • BF3: symmetric trigonal planar structure; its bond dipoles cancel. Pure BF3 has London forces, not permanent dipole-dipole attractions.
  • Polar bonds alone do not guarantee a polar molecule: consider the vector sum of the bond dipoles.

A permanent dipole can also induce a dipole in a neighbouring non-polar particle. In the broad convention used here, van der Waals forces include dispersion, permanent dipole-permanent dipole, and permanent dipole-induced dipole interactions. Terminology varies between courses; use the specific interaction name when possible.

4. Hydrogen bonding

In the usual A-level model, a hydrogen bond forms between an electron-deficient hydrogen covalently bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F in another molecule.

X—H ··· Y   (X and Y typically N, O or F)

The solid line is the covalent bond; the dotted line represents the hydrogen bond to the acceptor atom bearing a lone pair.

  • Water: O—H ··· O interactions.
  • Ammonia: N—H ··· N interactions.
  • Hydrogen fluoride: F—H ··· F interactions.
  • Among H2S, H2, NH3 and BF3, only NH3 forms conventional hydrogen bonds between its own molecules.
  • H2S has London and permanent dipole-dipole attractions. BF3 has no hydrogen-bond donor. Simply containing fluorine does not establish hydrogen bonding.

5. Water's unusual properties

  • Ice floats: hydrogen bonds stabilize an open lattice, so ice has a lower density than liquid water.
  • Melting: the open lattice is partly disrupted. Hydrogen bonds still exist in the liquid, continually breaking and reforming, and molecules pack more closely on average.
  • Relatively high melting and boiling points: compared with H2S, water's extensive hydrogen-bond network requires more energy to disrupt. Melting does not remove every hydrogen bond.
  • High surface tension: strong cohesion between molecules creates a net inward attraction at the surface. Increasing the surface area requires energy; the surface is not a fixed solid lattice.

6. Comparing boiling points

Stronger overall intermolecular attractions generally require more energy to overcome and produce a higher boiling point at the same pressure. A common guide for comparable small molecules is London forces < permanent dipole attractions < hydrogen bonding, but this is not a universal ranking. Polarizability, size, shape and the number of interactions matter.

CH4 (−162°C) < H2S (−60°C) < NH3 (−33°C)
  • CH4: London dispersion forces.
  • H2S: London forces and permanent dipole-dipole attractions.
  • NH3: London forces, permanent dipole interactions and hydrogen bonding.

The values above are the rounded boiling points quoted in the speech. H2O, HF and NH3 show higher boiling points than expected from dispersion-only trends because of hydrogen bonding; they need not have the highest boiling point of every hydride in their group.

Quiz: 40 Short Questions

1. What are intermolecular forces?

Show answer

Forces of attraction between molecules.

2. How do intermolecular forces differ from covalent bonds within a molecule?

Show answer

Intermolecular forces act between molecules; covalent bonds hold atoms together within a molecule.

3. Name the three main types of intermolecular attraction discussed in the speech.

Show answer

London dispersion forces, permanent dipole-dipole attractions, and hydrogen bonds.

4. What causes an instantaneous dipole?

Show answer

A momentary uneven distribution of electron density produces partial positive and negative regions.

5. How can an instantaneous dipole affect a neighbouring particle?

Show answer

It distorts the neighbour's electron cloud, inducing a dipole.

6. Explain how London dispersion forces arise.

Show answer

An instantaneous dipole induces a dipole in a neighbouring particle; the correlated opposite partial charges attract.

7. Are the dipoles responsible for London forces permanent?

Show answer

No. They fluctuate continually as electron distributions change.

8. Do London forces occur in both polar and non-polar molecules?

Show answer

Yes. Both types have electrons and experience London dispersion forces.

9. Which attractive forces act between noble-gas atoms?

Show answer

London dispersion forces.

10. Why do noble-gas boiling points generally increase down the group?

Show answer

Larger, more easily distorted electron clouds produce stronger London forces, so more energy is needed to separate the atoms.

11. What is a permanent molecular dipole?

Show answer

A persistent separation of partial positive and negative charge across a molecule.

12. Why is hydrogen chloride, HCl, polar?

Show answer

Chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen and attracts the shared electrons more strongly, producing a permanent bond dipole.

13. Identify the partial charges on hydrogen and chlorine in HCl.

Show answer

Hydrogen is delta positive; chlorine is delta negative.

14. How do permanent dipole-dipole attractions arise?

Show answer

The partial positive region of one polar molecule attracts the partial negative region of another.

15. What happens when like partial charges on neighbouring polar molecules face each other?

Show answer

They repel; molecular orientation affects the interaction.

16. Besides permanent dipole-dipole attractions, which forces occur between HCl molecules?

Show answer

London dispersion forces.

17. Can a polar molecule induce a dipole in a non-polar molecule?

Show answer

Yes. Its electric field can distort the non-polar molecule's electron cloud.

18. In the broad convention used here, what does van der Waals forces include?

Show answer

London dispersion, permanent dipole-permanent dipole, and permanent dipole-induced dipole interactions.

19. Does the presence of polar bonds guarantee that a molecule is polar?

Show answer

No. The bond dipoles may cancel because of the molecular geometry.

20. Why does carbon dioxide have no overall permanent dipole?

Show answer

Its linear, symmetric structure makes the two equal C=O bond dipoles cancel.

21. Why does carbon tetrachloride have no overall permanent dipole?

Show answer

Its four identical C-Cl bond dipoles cancel in its symmetric tetrahedral geometry.

22. Why is boron trifluoride, BF3, non-polar?

Show answer

Its three B-F bond dipoles cancel in a symmetric trigonal planar arrangement.

23. Which principal intermolecular attraction occurs between pure BF3 molecules?

Show answer

London dispersion forces; BF3 has no permanent molecular dipole.

24. Among HCl, CO2, CCl4 and BF3, which has permanent dipole-dipole attractions in its pure substance?

Show answer

HCl.

25. State the usual A-level conditions for an intermolecular hydrogen bond.

Show answer

Hydrogen bonded covalently to N, O or F is attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F in another molecule.

26. Why is hydrogen bonded to oxygen strongly delta positive?

Show answer

Oxygen attracts the shared bonding electrons strongly, leaving hydrogen electron deficient.

27. Describe a hydrogen bond between two water molecules.

Show answer

An O-H hydrogen on one water molecule is attracted to a lone pair on the oxygen of another molecule.

28. How is a hydrogen bond usually shown in a molecular diagram?

Show answer

As a dotted or dashed line between the donor hydrogen and the acceptor atom.

29. Name three substances from the speech that form intermolecular hydrogen bonds.

Show answer

Water (H2O), ammonia (NH3), and hydrogen fluoride (HF).

30. Which of H2S, H2, NH3 and BF3 forms conventional hydrogen bonds between its own molecules?

Show answer

NH3: it has N-H bonds and a lone pair on nitrogen.

31. Why does H2 not form hydrogen bonds between its molecules?

Show answer

Its hydrogen atoms are not bonded to N, O or F, and the molecule has no suitable lone-pair acceptor.

32. Why does pure BF3 not form hydrogen bonds between its molecules?

Show answer

It contains no hydrogen-bond donor: there is no hydrogen bonded to N, O or F.

33. Which intermolecular forces occur in hydrogen sulfide, H2S, at this level?

Show answer

London dispersion and permanent dipole-dipole forces, but not conventional N/O/F hydrogen bonding.

34. Is the order London forces < permanent dipole forces < hydrogen bonds always valid?

Show answer

No. It is a useful rough guide for comparable small molecules; size, polarizability, shape and the number of interactions also matter.

35. Which attractions are overcome when water boils: hydrogen bonds between molecules or O-H covalent bonds within molecules?

Show answer

Intermolecular attractions, including hydrogen bonds, are overcome; the O-H covalent bonds remain intact.

36. Why is ice less dense than liquid water?

Show answer

Hydrogen bonding holds water molecules in an open lattice in ice; liquid water packs more closely on average.

37. What happens to water's hydrogen-bond network when ice melts?

Show answer

The open lattice is partly disrupted; hydrogen bonds continue to break and reform as molecules move closer together on average.

38. Why does water have a higher boiling point than hydrogen sulfide?

Show answer

Water forms an extensive hydrogen-bond network, requiring more energy to overcome than the intermolecular attractions in H2S.

39. How does hydrogen bonding contribute to water's high surface tension?

Show answer

It strengthens cohesion between water molecules; surface molecules experience a net inward attraction, making surface expansion energetically costly.

40. Arrange CH4 (-162 C), H2S (-60 C), and NH3 (-33 C) in increasing boiling point and explain the order.

Show answer

CH4 < H2S < NH3. CH4 has London forces; H2S also has permanent dipole attractions; NH3 additionally forms hydrogen bonds. For these substances, the overall attractions require progressively more energy to overcome.
