ALCH11. Covalent Bonding and Structures

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

Covalent Bonds and Dot-and-Cross Diagrams
  • A covalent bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the bonded atoms.
  • Covalent bonding usually occurs between non-metal atoms.
  • Dots and crosses identify the atoms from which electrons originate; they do not represent different kinds of electron.
  • Diagrams usually show outer-shell electrons. Sharing can give atoms full outer shells: two electrons for hydrogen and eight for the examples discussed.
  • Chlorine has the electron arrangement 2,8,7. In Cl2, each atom contributes one electron to a shared pair.
Single and Multiple Covalent Bonds
  • A single bond contains one shared electron pair. Cl2 has one single bond; H2O has two O-H single bonds.
  • Oxygen has six outer-shell electrons. In water, oxygen forms two bonds and retains two lone pairs.
  • A double bond contains two shared pairs, as in O2.
  • A triple bond contains three shared pairs, or six electrons, as in N2. Nitrogen begins with five outer-shell electrons.
Dative or Coordinate Bonds
  • In a dative covalent bond, both electrons in the shared pair originate from the same atom.
  • An arrow points from the electron-pair donor to the acceptor.
  • Ammonia, NH3, donates the nitrogen lone pair to H+ to form NH4+. The ammonium ion has an overall charge of +1.
  • Once formed, the four N-H bonds in NH4+ are equivalent.
  • In the dot-and-cross model for CO, the triple bond includes a pair donated by oxygen to carbon. Each atom also has one lone pair.
Simple Molecular Substances
  • These substances consist of small, discrete molecules. Their atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds; neighbouring molecules attract through intermolecular forces.
  • In the solid state, the molecules form a molecular lattice. Solid sulfur consists of S8 molecules arranged in such a lattice.
  • Melting and boiling generally require relatively little energy to overcome intermolecular forces; the covalent bonds within the molecules remain intact.
  • Neutral simple molecular substances generally do not conduct electricity because they lack mobile charge carriers.
  • Solubility depends on both solute and solvent: non-polar molecules often dissolve in non-polar solvents. Some polar molecules dissolve in polar solvents; water is an important example.
Giant Covalent Structures
  • A giant covalent structure is an extended network of atoms joined by strong covalent bonds, rather than separate small molecules.
  • Examples include diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide (silica), SiO2.
  • High melting points reflect the large energy required to break many strong covalent bonds.
  • These structures are generally insoluble in ordinary polar and non-polar solvents.
  • Diamond and silica do not conduct electricity. Graphite conducts because it has mobile delocalised electrons.
Diamond, Graphite and Silica
  • Diamond: each carbon bonds to four other carbon atoms in a rigid three-dimensional network. It is very hard and has no mobile charge carriers.
  • Graphite: each carbon bonds to three others in layers. Weak attractions between layers allow them to slide, making graphite soft and slippery.
  • Graphite's delocalised electrons move along the layers and carry electric current.
  • Silica: silicon and oxygen form a giant covalent network. It is hard, has a high melting point and does not conduct electricity.
Exam Focus: Explain Properties Using Bonding
  • Define a covalent bond using electrostatic attraction, a shared electron pair and the bonded nuclei.
  • For high melting points, identify strong covalent bonds throughout a giant network and the large energy needed to break them.
  • For conductivity, explain whether charged particles are free to move.
  • Ice melts by overcoming attractions between water molecules; melting silica requires disruption of its covalent network. This explains silica's much higher melting point.
  • Solid sulfur is a molecular lattice containing discrete S8 molecules, not a giant covalent or ionic lattice.
