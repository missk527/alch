Notes

1. Predicting molecular and ionic shapes

Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory predicts shapes from the number and type of electron domains around a central atom. Domains repel one another and arrange themselves as far apart as possible.

  • A bonding pair is shared in a covalent bond. A lone pair is not involved in bonding.
  • Each single, double, or triple bond counts as one electron domain for predicting geometry. Each lone pair also counts as one domain.
  • Repulsion strength: lone pair-lone pair > lone pair-bonding pair > bonding pair-bonding pair.
  • Lone pairs occupy more space around the central atom and can compress bond angles.
  • Electron-domain geometry includes lone pairs; molecular shape describes the positions of atoms only.

2. Representing three-dimensional structures

  • Ordinary line: bond in the plane of the paper.
  • Solid wedge: bond projecting towards the viewer.
  • Dashed wedge: bond projecting away from the viewer.

3. The octet rule and its exceptions

  • Many atoms achieve eight valence-shell electrons through bonding, but the octet rule has exceptions.
  • Incomplete octets: beryllium and boron can form compounds with fewer than eight electrons around the central atom. In BF3, boron has three bonding pairs and six electrons around it.
  • Expanded octets: some period 3 and heavier elements form species represented with more than eight electrons around the central atom. PCl5 has five bonding pairs around phosphorus; SF6 has six around sulfur.
  • In SF6, sulfur is surrounded by twelve electrons in six bonding pairs. Oxygen is in period 2 and cannot expand its octet to form OF6 in the same way.

5. Explaining shapes

  • CO2: two double bonds form two domains around carbon. With no central lone pairs, they arrange 180° apart.
  • NH3: three bonding pairs and one lone pair give a trigonal pyramidal molecular shape. The lone pair compresses the angle below 109.5°.
  • H2O: two bonding pairs and two lone pairs give a bent shape and an angle of about 104.5°.
  • PF3 and NF3: each has three bonding pairs and one lone pair around the central atom. Their molecular shape is trigonal pyramidal, while the electron-domain arrangement is tetrahedral.
  • Hydrazine, N2H4: each nitrogen has two N-H bonds, one N-N bond, and one lone pair. The lesson's simplified model predicts an H-N-H angle near 107°.

Accuracy note: The 2.5° reduction per lone pair is a classroom approximation, not a universal rule. VSEPR predicts approximate angles; PF3 and NF3 should not automatically be assigned the same measured angle as NH3.

6. Exam method

  1. Draw the Lewis structure. Add electrons for a negative ionic charge or remove electrons for a positive charge.
  2. Count electron domains around the central atom and identify lone pairs.
  3. Use the arrangement that minimises repulsion.
  4. Name the molecular shape from atom positions and give an appropriate ideal or approximate angle.
  5. Explain any compression using stronger lone-pair repulsion.

Reference for clarification of VSEPR and electron domains: OpenStax Chemistry 2e, section 7.6.

Quiz: 40 Short Questions

Use VSEPR theory to explain molecular shapes and approximate bond angles. Distinguish molecular shape from electron-domain geometry.

1. What two features determine the shape around a central atom?

Show answer

The number of electron domains and whether they are bonding domains or lone pairs.

2. What is a bonding pair of electrons?

Show answer

A pair of electrons shared between atoms in a covalent bond.

3. What is a lone pair of electrons?

Show answer

A pair of valence electrons not involved in bonding.

4. State the main principle of VSEPR theory.

Show answer

Electron domains repel one another and arrange themselves to minimise repulsion.

5. Which causes stronger repulsion: a lone pair or a bonding pair?

Show answer

A lone pair, because its electron density occupies more space around the central atom.

6. State the order of repulsion strengths between pairs.

Show answer

Lone pair-lone pair > lone pair-bonding pair > bonding pair-bonding pair.

7. What does an ordinary line represent in a three-dimensional molecular drawing?

Show answer

A bond in the plane of the paper.

8. What does a solid wedge represent?

Show answer

A bond projecting out of the paper towards the viewer.

9. What does a dashed wedge represent?

Show answer

A bond projecting behind the paper, away from the viewer.

10. State the octet rule.

Show answer

Many atoms achieve eight electrons in their valence shell through bonding; exceptions exist.

11. What is an incomplete octet?

Show answer

A valence shell containing fewer than eight electrons around an atom in a bonded species.

12. How many electrons surround boron in BF3?

Show answer

Six electrons, in three bonding pairs.

13. Why is BF3 an exception to the octet rule?

Show answer

Boron has only six electrons around it rather than eight.

14. What is an expanded octet?

Show answer

More than eight electrons around a central atom in its Lewis structure.

15. Give an example of a molecule with an expanded octet.

Show answer

SF6 or PCl5.

16. How many bonding pairs and electrons surround sulfur in SF6?

Show answer

Six bonding pairs, representing twelve electrons.

17. Why can oxygen not form OF6 in the same way that sulfur forms SF6?

Show answer

Oxygen is a period 2 element and cannot expand its octet.

18. How many electron domains does a double bond count as in VSEPR?

Show answer

One electron domain, even though it contains two shared electron pairs.

19. What is the shape of a diatomic molecule such as H2?

Show answer

Linear; with only two atoms, there is no bond angle to define.

20. State the shape and bond angle of CO2.

Show answer

Linear, with an O-C-O bond angle of 180 degrees.

21. Why is CO2 linear despite containing double bonds?

Show answer

The two C=O bonds form two electron domains around carbon, with no lone pairs; the domains arrange 180 degrees apart.

22. State the shape and bond angle for three bonding domains and no lone pairs.

Show answer

Trigonal planar, 120 degrees.

23. State the shape and F-B-F bond angle of BF3.

Show answer

Trigonal planar, 120 degrees.

24. State the shape and bond angle for four bonding domains and no lone pairs.

Show answer

Tetrahedral, approximately 109.5 degrees.

25. State the shape and H-C-H bond angle of CH4.

Show answer

Tetrahedral, approximately 109.5 degrees.

26. How many bonding pairs and lone pairs surround nitrogen in NH3?

Show answer

Three bonding pairs and one lone pair.

27. State the molecular shape and approximate bond angle of NH3.

Show answer

Trigonal pyramidal, approximately 107 degrees.

28. Why is the bond angle in NH3 smaller than in CH4?

Show answer

The lone pair in NH3 repels bonding pairs more strongly, compressing the H-N-H angles.

29. How many bonding pairs and lone pairs surround oxygen in H2O?

Show answer

Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs.

30. State the molecular shape and approximate bond angle of H2O.

Show answer

Bent (non-linear), approximately 104.5 degrees.

31. Why is the bond angle in H2O smaller than in NH3?

Show answer

Water has two lone pairs around its central atom; their stronger repulsions compress the bonding angle further.

32. Is a reduction of 2.5 degrees per lone pair a universal rule?

Show answer

No. It is a simplified estimate for familiar examples such as NH3 and H2O; actual angles depend on the molecule.

33. State the shape of PCl5.

Show answer

Trigonal bipyramidal.

34. State the equatorial-equatorial and axial-equatorial angles in PCl5.

Show answer

120 degrees and 90 degrees, respectively; the axial-axial angle is 180 degrees.

35. State the shape and adjacent F-S-F bond angle of SF6.

Show answer

Octahedral, 90 degrees. Opposite bonds are 180 degrees apart.

36. How many bonding pairs and lone pairs surround each nitrogen in hydrazine, N2H4?

Show answer

Three bonding pairs and one lone pair: two N-H bonds, one N-N bond, and one lone pair.

37. Using the simplified lesson model, predict the H-N-H angle around each nitrogen in hydrazine.

Show answer

Approximately 107 degrees: each nitrogen has three bonding pairs and one lone pair. This is a model estimate, not an exact measured value.

38. State the molecular shape and electron-domain geometry of PF3.

Show answer

The molecular shape is trigonal pyramidal; its four electron domains have a tetrahedral arrangement.

39. Why do BF3 and NF3 have different molecular shapes?

Show answer

BF3 has three bonding domains and no lone pair on boron, so it is trigonal planar. NF3 has three bonding domains and one lone pair on nitrogen, so it is trigonal pyramidal.

40. Describe a method for predicting a molecule or ion's shape.

Show answer

Draw its Lewis structure, account for any ionic charge, count electron domains around the central atom, identify lone pairs, and choose the arrangement that minimises repulsion. Name the shape from the atom positions.
