ALC4. Mass Spectrometry

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

Introduction | Mass Spectrometry
  • Mass spectrometry is a form of molecular chemical analysis.
  • The sample is ionised, the ions are separated according to their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and the ions are detected to produce a mass spectrum.
  • Mass spectra provide structural information, identify unknown compounds, and determine the relative abundances of isotopes.
Time-of-Flight Apparatus
  • The apparatus contains a sample inlet, a flight tube, and a detector near the end of the tube.
  • It is kept under vacuum so that ions do not collide with air particles.
Ionisation
  • The sample is dissolved in a volatile solvent, which vaporises easily.
  • The solution is forced through a hollow needle connected to the positive terminal of a high-voltage supply.
  • Tiny positively charged droplets form and lose electrons.
  • As the solvent evaporates, the droplets become smaller until single positively charged ions remain.
Acceleration and Flight
  • Positive ions are attracted towards a negatively charged electric plate and accelerate towards it.
  • All ions receive the same kinetic energy: heavier ions travel more slowly and lighter ions travel faster.
  • The ions pass through a hole in the plate, form a beam, and travel along the flight tube.
  • Their time of flight is recorded when they reach the detector.
Detection and the Mass Spectrum
  • At the detector, positive ions gain electrons and cause an electric current to flow.
  • The current produces a signal that is sent to a computer, which generates a mass spectrum.
Low-resolution Mass Spectrometry
  • Mass spectrometry can measure values to five decimal places; measurements to one decimal place are described as low resolution.
  • Isotopes have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
  • Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 both contain 17 protons; they contain 18 and 20 neutrons respectively.
  • Their different masses give different m/z values, so they are detected separately.
Reading a Chlorine Mass Spectrum
  • Chlorine produces peaks at m/z 35 and 37 for chlorine-35 and chlorine-37.
  • Peak height represents relative isotopic abundance.
  • In the example, chlorine-35 has 75% abundance and chlorine-37 has 25% abundance.
Calculating Relative Atomic Mass
  • Multiply each isotopic mass by its percentage abundance, add the products, and divide by 100.
  • For chlorine: [(35 × 75) + (37 × 25)] ÷ 100 = 35.5.
  • The result is closer to 35 because chlorine-35 is more abundant.
  • For element X: [(24 × 78.7) + (25 × 10.13) + (26 × 11.17)] ÷ 100 = 24.3247, or 24.32 to two decimal places.
  • An element with a relative atomic mass of about 24.3 is magnesium.
Process and Exam Skills
  • After vaporisation and before detection, particles undergo ionisation, acceleration, and separation according to m/z.
  • In the exam example, the separation stage is described as deflection.
  • Always follow the requested number of decimal places in calculation questions.
