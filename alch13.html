Electronegativity and Polarity

Notes

1. Covalent bonds and electronegativity

A covalent bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the bonded atoms.

Electronegativity is the ability of a bonded atom to attract the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond. It is measured using the Pauling scale, developed by Linus Pauling.

2. Trends in electronegativity

  • Across a period: electronegativity generally increases from left to right. Nuclear charge increases while shielding remains broadly similar, strengthening attraction for bonding electrons.
  • Down a group: electronegativity generally decreases. Extra electron shells increase distance and shielding, weakening attraction for bonding electrons.
  • Fluorine is the most electronegative element. It is more electronegative than bromine.

3. Polar and non-polar bonds

  • Non-polar covalent bonds: bonding electrons are shared equally or approximately equally. Identical atoms, such as the atoms in H2 or Cl2, have equal electronegativities.
  • Polar covalent bonds: different electronegativities cause unequal sharing of the bonding electrons.
  • The more electronegative atom attracts more electron density and becomes δ−; the other end becomes δ+.
  • These are partial charges, not full ionic charges. A persistent charge separation is a permanent dipole.
Hδ+ — Clδ−

Using the lesson's rounded Pauling values, H = 2.1 and Cl = 3.0, the electronegativity difference is 0.9. Chlorine attracts the shared electrons more strongly.

4. Electron density and the bonding spectrum

Electron density describes the distribution of electrons in space and relates to the probability of finding electrons in a region. Bonding electron density is shared equally in H2 but shifted towards chlorine in HCl.

Non-polar covalent → Polar covalent → Predominantly ionic

Bonding forms a continuum. A larger electronegativity difference generally gives greater bond polarity and ionic character; these categories are useful models rather than perfectly separate boxes.

5. Bond polarity versus molecular polarity

A molecule with polar bonds is not necessarily polar overall. Molecular polarity depends on the three-dimensional arrangement and the vector sum of its dipoles.

  • Polar molecule: dipoles do not cancel; there is an overall permanent dipole moment.
  • Non-polar molecule: there is no overall permanent dipole. Polar bond dipoles may cancel through their arrangement.
  • Do not use shape alone: the identities of the surrounding atoms also matter.

6. Key molecular examples

  • H2O: two bonding pairs and two lone pairs around oxygen; bent shape, about 104.5°. Its O-H dipoles do not cancel, so it is polar.
  • CO2: linear, 180°. Its two equal C=O dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel, so it is non-polar.
  • BF3: trigonal planar. Its three equal B-F dipoles cancel, so it is non-polar.
  • NF3 and NH3: trigonal pyramidal. Their dipoles do not cancel completely, so they are polar.
  • SiCl4: tetrahedral with four identical surrounding atoms. Its dipoles cancel, so it is non-polar.
  • CH2Cl2: tetrahedral with two H atoms and two Cl atoms. Its dipoles do not cancel, so it is polar. Each C-Cl bond has Cδ+ and Clδ−.

7. Exam method

  1. Define electronegativity using a bonded atom and the shared pair of electrons.
  2. Identify polar bonds and assign partial charges using electronegativity.
  3. Determine the three-dimensional molecular shape, including the effects of lone pairs.
  4. Decide whether the bond dipoles cancel and explain the overall polarity.
  5. For a 3D drawing, use ordinary lines for bonds in the page, a solid wedge towards the viewer, and a dashed wedge away from the viewer.

Precision note: bent and trigonal pyramidal molecules can possess symmetry while still being polar. The decisive point is whether their dipoles cancel, not whether the molecule has any symmetry at all. Different elements can also have very similar electronegativities, so a bond between different atoms is not always appreciably polar.
